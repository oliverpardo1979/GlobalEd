\begin{abstract}
This paper separates changes in average monthly labor income into changes in
educational attainment and changes in income within education groups. We use
harmonized aggregates from the World Bank's LAC Equity Lab and construct
endpoint-year comparisons that retain the same observed quarters, survey, and
series. Between 2016 and 2019, the fixed-employment-weight synthetic mean
income rises 1.2 percent among 13 covered economies. Educational upgrading
contributes an amount equivalent to 3.6 percent of initial mean income, while
income changes within education groups subtract 2.4 percent. Between 2019 and
2023, the corresponding contributions are 4.1 and -2.9 percent, producing a
1.2 percent increase among 9 economies. These are accounting results for a
covered-economy sample, not causal returns to education or estimates for Latin
America as a whole.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Average labor income can increase because the employed population attains
higher levels of education or because workers with a given educational
attainment receive higher income. Educational upgrading changes the
composition of employment. Income changes within education groups alter the
remuneration associated with the observed education categories. The two
components are exactly additive but have different economic interpretations.

This paper measures both contributions for Latin American economies covered
by the World Bank's LAC Equity Lab. It compares 2016 with 2019 to describe
pre-pandemic change, 2019 with 2023 to describe the pandemic and recovery
period, and 2016 with 2023 to measure the longer change. Each comparison uses
only the two endpoint years.

The analysis differs from the companion global paper in its population and
income concept. LABLAC measures monthly labor income for all employed workers
with coherent reported income, including employees and self-employed workers.
The global paper instead measures wages among employees. The two samples
therefore should not be pooled.

\section{Related literature}

The method follows \citet{kitagawa1955} and the decomposition literature
reviewed by \citet{fortinlemieuxfirpo2011}. The substantive discussion builds
on research documenting educational upgrading, changing skill premia, and
labor income inequality in Latin America
\citep{gasparinietal2011,azevedoetal2013,messinadasilva2019}.

\section{Data}

\subsection{LAC Equity Lab}

The World Bank's LAC Equity Lab provides mean monthly labor income and worker
counts for three harmonized educational-attainment groups: low, middle, and
high \citep{lablac}. The downloaded package contains Argentina, Bolivia,
Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Costa Rica, the Dominican Republic, Ecuador, El
Salvador, Guatemala, Mexico, Paraguay, Peru (Lima and Callao), and Uruguay.
The Peruvian observation is subnational and should not be interpreted as a
national estimate.

Monthly labor income is reported in 2017 purchasing-power-parity dollars. The
income measure covers workers aged 15 or older with coherent reported income.
The available worker counts cover all workers in the corresponding education
group. The analysis therefore defines a synthetic mean that weights the three
education-group income means with the published worker counts. This mean is
exactly decomposable but need not reproduce LABLAC's published total.

\subsection{Endpoint pairs and matched quarters}

A comparison requires the initial and final year, but does not require
observations in intervening years. Within each endpoint year, the calculation
retains only quarters observed in both years for the same economy, survey, and
Tableau series. Argentina, for example, uses the second through fourth
quarters in the 2016--2019 comparison. Chile and Guatemala use the fourth
quarter, while most other economies use all four quarters.

The Tableau documentation states that a change in series represents a survey
methodological change. The 2016--2019 sample contains 13 economies; only
Paraguay lacks a 2016 endpoint. The 2019--2023 and 2016--2023 samples contain
9 economies: Argentina, Bolivia, Brazil, Chile, Costa Rica, the Dominican
Republic, El Salvador, Mexico, and Peru (Lima and Callao). Colombia, Ecuador,
Paraguay, and Uruguay change series between the endpoints, and Guatemala has
no 2023 observation.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Construction of endpoint-year measures}

Let \(Q_c\) denote the quarters observed for economy \(c\) in both endpoint
years. For education group \(e\), the endpoint-year income and worker count
are
\begin{equation}
  r_{ce,t}
  =
  \frac{\sum_{q\in Q_c} N_{ce,tq}r_{ce,tq}}
       {\sum_{q\in Q_c} N_{ce,tq}},
  \qquad
  N_{ce,t}
  =
  \frac{1}{|Q_c|}
  \sum_{q\in Q_c}N_{ce,tq}.
  \label{eq:latam_endpoint_aggregation}
\end{equation}
Using the same quarters at both endpoints prevents changes in observed survey
frequency or seasonality from entering the decomposition mechanically.

Between 2016 and 2019, synthetic mean labor income rises 1.2 percent.
Educational upgrading contributes 3.6 percent of initial mean income. Income
changes within economy--education cells subtract 2.4 percent. Educational
upgrading therefore more than accounts for the net increase and offsets a
decline in income within education groups.

Between 2019 and 2023, synthetic mean labor income also rises 1.2 percent
among the 9 covered economies. Educational upgrading contributes 4.1 percent
of initial mean income, while within-group income changes subtract 2.9
percent. Over the longer 2016--2023 window, synthetic mean income rises 2.3
percent, split into a 7.8 percent education contribution and a -5.5 percent
within-group contribution.

\subsection{Country weighting and heterogeneity}

Allowing economy worker shares to change has little effect on the fixed-weight
results. The 2016--2019 increase becomes 1.1 percent, with a -0.1 percent
between-economy composition component. The corresponding 2019--2023 increase
is 1.1 percent, and the between-economy component is close to zero.

Equal economy weights produce lower changes: 0.5 percent in 2016--2019 and
-3.0 percent in 2019--2023. Brazil and Mexico account for 66 percent of
midpoint worker weight in the first window and 78 percent in the second.
The difference between weighting schemes therefore reflects sample
concentration rather than a discrepancy in the decomposition.

Country changes also vary substantially. Between 2016 and 2019, synthetic
mean income rises 12.9 percent in El Salvador and falls 12.4 percent in
Argentina. Between 2019 and 2023, Mexico records the largest increase, 8.6
percent, while Argentina records the largest decline, 12.5 percent. These
comparisons are descriptive and do not identify the mechanisms behind the
country differences.

\section{Robustness and limitations}

The synthetic mean closely approximates LABLAC's published total but is not
identical to it. Across selected endpoint pairs, the maximum absolute
reconstruction difference is 4.4 percent for income and 0.4 percent for
worker counts. The decomposition remains exactly additive because the
synthetic mean, rather than the published total, defines the estimand.

The results are also sensitive to economy weighting. Under fixed worker
weights, Brazil and Mexico have a large influence on the covered-economy mean.
Equal weighting gives the same qualitative decomposition---a positive
education component and a negative within-group component---but produces a
negative total change after 2019.

Coverage is limited. Peru covers Lima and Callao. Chile and Guatemala use
fourth-quarter rather than full-year comparisons in 2016--2019. The 2023
samples also exclude four economies with documented series changes. These
restrictions prevent interpreting the aggregate as representative of Latin
America.

\section{Conclusion}

Educational upgrading increased synthetic mean labor income in every
comparison window. Income changes within education groups moved in the
opposite direction and offset most of that contribution. For the
employment-weighted covered sample, the resulting increase was 1.2 percent
before the pandemic, 1.2 percent between 2019 and 2023, and 2.3 percent over
2016--2023.

The results establish an accounting pattern rather than a causal return to
education. They show that recent growth in average labor income among the
covered economies depended on a more educated employment composition while
income within education groups weakened. Extending that conclusion to Latin
America as a whole requires comparable endpoints for more economies and
worker counts that match the income-reporting population exactly.
