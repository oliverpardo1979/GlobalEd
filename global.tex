\begin{abstract}
This paper separates changes in average employee wages into changes in
educational attainment and changes in wages within education groups. We
combine annual ILOSTAT series on average monthly earnings and employee counts
for four harmonized education groups with World Bank price and
purchasing-power-parity data. The common 2015--2019 window contains 17
countries after source-consistency and data-quality screens. With country
weights fixed, the covered-country mean real wage rises 6.2 percent.
Educational upgrading contributes an amount equivalent to 1.7 percent of the
initial mean wage, while wage changes within education groups contribute 4.4
percent. Allowing country employment shares to change lowers the observed
increase to 5.0 percent because the between-country composition component
subtracts 1.1 percent. These are accounting results for the covered-country
sample, not causal returns to education or estimates for the world as a whole.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Average wages can rise because workers attain higher levels of education or
because employees with a given educational attainment receive higher wages.
Educational upgrading changes the composition of employment. Wage growth
within education groups changes the remuneration attached to the observed
education categories. The two components are exactly additive but have
different economic interpretations.

This paper measures both contributions within countries and for a common
international sample. It asks three questions. First, how much of each
country's change in average real wages is associated with educational
upgrading? Second, how much is associated with wage changes within education
groups? Third, what do these components imply for the covered-country mean
when country weights are held fixed or allowed to change?

The analysis uses annual ILOSTAT data for employees
\citep{ilostat2026}. This restriction is substantive: the earnings series
refers to employees, so its population weights must also refer to employees.
We match average monthly earnings by education to employment by education and
status in employment, retaining the same country, year, and statistical
source. The aggregate estimates are reported as results for the
covered-country sample because the available balanced panels do not represent
the world as a whole.

\section{Related literature}

The accounting framework follows the symmetric decomposition proposed by
\citet{kitagawa1955}. It is related to the broader decomposition literature
reviewed by \citet{fortinlemieuxfirpo2011}, but differs from regression-based
Oaxaca--Blinder exercises because it decomposes the observed mean directly.
The paper also relates to evidence on compositional effects in aggregate wage
growth \citep{dalyhobijn2017,kouvavasetal2019} and to studies of educational
upgrading and skill premia.

\section{Data}

\subsection{ILOSTAT earnings and employment}

The remuneration measure is average nominal monthly earnings of employees by
sex and education. Population weights come from employment by sex, status in
employment, and education. The baseline sample retains total sex, employees,
and four aggregate education groups: less than basic, basic, intermediate, and
advanced.

The two tables are matched by country, year, and source. The initial audit
finds no duplicate observations at that grain. It also compares the weighted
average of the four education groups with ILOSTAT's published total and
records the share of employees whose education is not stated.

\subsection{Prices and international comparability}

Nominal earnings are converted to constant 2021 international dollars with
the national consumer price index and the 2021 private-consumption
purchasing-power-parity conversion factor from the World Development
Indicators \citep{worldbank2026wdi,worldbank2026cpi,worldbank2026ppp}. For
country \(c\) in year \(t\), the conversion is
\begin{equation}
  r_{ce,t}^{2021\,PPP}
  =
  r_{ce,t}^{nominal}
  \frac{CPI_{c,2021}}{CPI_{c,t}}
  \frac{1}{PPP_{c,2021}}.
  \label{eq:ppp_conversion}
\end{equation}
Private-consumption PPP is preferred to GDP PPP because the comparison
concerns the purchasing power of employee earnings.

\subsection{Common-window samples}

A country-source series must contain every year in the comparison window and
pass the quality screen in every year. The reconstructed total must be within
5 percent of ILOSTAT's published total, and employees with education not
stated may account for at most 5 percent of the total. The source, reported
currency marker, and data-reference period must remain unchanged, and the
window cannot cross a break reported by ILOSTAT.

ILOSTAT does not mark every apparent classification break. The baseline
therefore excludes a series if any education group's employment share changes
by more than 10 percentage points in one year. Results are also calculated
without this screen and with a stricter 5-point threshold. After the baseline
screen, the common samples contain 8 countries in 2010--2019, 17 in
2015--2019, and 11 in 2019--2023.

\subsection{Comparison windows}

The 2010--2019 window measures longer-run change before the pandemic but has
the narrowest coverage. The 2015--2019 window is the baseline because it
preserves a common pre-pandemic interval while more than doubling the number
of countries. The 2019--2023 window describes the combined pandemic and
recovery period. Comparisons do not cross reported breaks in series.

\subsection{Aggregate decomposition for the covered countries}

Between 2015 and 2019, the fixed-employment-weight mean wage rises 6.2
percent. Educational upgrading contributes 1.7 percent of the initial mean,
or about 28 percent of the total increase. Wage changes within
country--education cells contribute 4.4 percent, or about 72 percent of the
increase. With equal country weights, the total increase is 7.2 percent:
educational upgrading contributes 1.7 percent and within-group wage changes
contribute 5.6 percent.

Allowing employment weights to change reduces the 2015--2019 increase from
6.2 to 5.0 percent. The educational-upgrading and within-group wage components
remain close to 1.7 and 4.4 percent, while the changing country mix subtracts
1.1 percent. This last component records changes in countries' shares of
sample employment. It does not identify migration or any other single
mechanism.

The longer 2010--2019 window produces an 8.0 percent fixed-weight increase,
split into 3.7 percent from educational upgrading and 4.3 percent from wage
changes within education groups. This estimate covers only eight countries,
and the United States accounts for about two-thirds of their initial
employment. The 2019--2023 fixed-weight increase is 3.6 percent among 11
countries. Educational upgrading contributes 2.5 percent and within-group
wage changes contribute 1.1 percent.

\subsection{Country heterogeneity}

The aggregate results combine substantially different country experiences.
The fixed-employment specification gives more weight to countries with more
employees, while the equal-weight specification describes the mean country.
The difference between the two is therefore informative about sample
concentration, but neither weighting scheme turns the available countries into
a representative world sample.

\section{Robustness and data-quality sensitivity}

The attribution between components is sensitive to unreported education
classification breaks. Brazil illustrates the problem in the 2015--2019
window: the share of employees with less than basic education falls from 36.5
percent in 2015 to 7.3 percent in 2016, while the advanced share rises from
5.0 to 19.8 percent. ILOSTAT does not flag that change as a break.

Without the education-share jump screen, the 2015--2019 fixed-weight increase
is 6.4 percent, but educational upgrading contributes 4.2 percent and
within-group wage changes contribute 2.2 percent. Under the stricter
5-percentage-point screen, the total increase is 6.0 percent, split into 1.8
percent from educational upgrading and 4.2 percent from within-group wage
changes. The baseline 10-point screen therefore changes the attribution more
than the total. This sensitivity is reported explicitly rather than treating
the unmarked break as observed educational upgrading.

\section{Conclusion}

For the 17 countries observed consistently between 2015 and 2019, wage changes
within education groups account for most of the increase in the fixed-weight
mean real wage. Educational upgrading remains positive, but contributes less
than one-third of the total increase. The relative contribution of educational
upgrading is larger in the smaller 2010--2019 and 2019--2023 samples.

These estimates establish that the decomposition can be aggregated across
countries without losing exact additivity. They do not yet establish a world
result. Expanding country coverage and resolving unreported classification
breaks are necessary before interpreting the covered-country estimates as
evidence about global wage growth.
